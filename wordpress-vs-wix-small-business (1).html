WordPress vs Wix for Small Business: Pros, Cons, and Which Wins

By Afolabi Oluwaseyi Christopher · July 2026 · 5 min read
Wix wins for small businesses that want a simple site live in days with no ongoing technical management; WordPress wins for businesses planning to grow content, SEO, or functionality significantly over the next few years.

Start with your actual timeline

If you need a website live this week and don't plan to touch it much afterward, Wix's drag-and-drop simplicity is genuinely the faster path. If you're building something you expect to actively grow — more pages, more blog content, more integrations — WordPress's flexibility pays off over time even though it takes longer to set up properly.

Ease of use

Wix is designed so a non-technical person can build and edit their own site with minimal help. WordPress has a steeper learning curve, especially for anything beyond basic text and image edits — most small business owners end up hiring someone for the initial build and any structural changes later.

SEO and growth potential

WordPress generally gives more control over technical SEO — page structure, plugin choices, site speed optimization, and content organization. Wix has improved significantly here in recent years and is perfectly capable for most small businesses, but WordPress remains the stronger choice if search ranking is a core part of your growth strategy.

Cost comparison

Wix plans typically run $16-$59/month all-in, with hosting included. WordPress itself is free, but you'll pay for hosting ($5-$30/month typically), and possibly premium plugins or themes — often landing in a similar total monthly range, just split across more line items.

What actually breaks this tie

Ask yourself: will this site mostly stay as-is once it's built, or will it become a growing hub of content, products, and functionality? Static and simple → Wix. Growing and content-driven → WordPress.

Can I move from Wix to WordPress later?

Yes, but it means rebuilding rather than a direct import — Wix doesn't export cleanly into WordPress's format. It's a real migration project, not a quick switch.

Is WordPress harder to maintain long-term?

It requires occasional updates (plugins, WordPress core, security patches), which is why many small businesses pay a developer for ongoing maintenance rather than doing it themselves.
